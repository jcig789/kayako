How Philippine Banks Actually Compute Your DSR
If you take away one number from this whole site, make it this one. Net DSR decides loan approval more than your salary, your down payment, or the property price on its own.
Home & Condo September 19, 2026

What DSR actually means

DSR stands for Debt Service Ratio — the share of your monthly income that goes toward debt payments. For a home loan, that's your mortgage payment (plus any existing loans you're already paying) divided by your income.

DSR = (monthly loan payment + existing loan payments) ÷ income

Simple on paper. The part that trips people up — and the part that makes online "loan amount ÷ years" calculators unreliable — is what goes on the bottom of that fraction.

Net income, not gross

Banks and Pag-IBIG don't compute DSR against your gross salary. They compute it against what you actually take home — after income tax, SSS, PhilHealth, and Pag-IBIG contributions are deducted. That difference is bigger than most people expect.

Example: someone earning ₱80,000/month gross, employed (not self-employed or OFW), takes home roughly ₱64,000–65,000/month after TRAIN Law income tax and mandatory contributions — about 19–20% gone before it ever reaches their pocket. A calculator that runs DSR against the ₱80,000 figure will show a healthier-looking ratio than what a bank actually applies.

This is exactly why gross-based calculators overstate what you can afford. The gap between gross and net widens further depending on employment type — see below.

Employment type changes the math, a lot

Tax and mandatory-deduction treatment differs meaningfully by how you're employed, which changes your real net income and therefore your real DSR:

Employed

Standard TRAIN Law graduated income tax on annual taxable income, plus employee-share SSS (5%), PhilHealth (2.5%), and Pag-IBIG contributions.

Self-employed

Can elect the 8% flat tax on gross receipts above ₱250,000/year instead of graduated tax — often a meaningfully different number than an employee at the same income. SSS contributions are higher (15% of monthly salary credit, since there's no employer share to split it with). Banks typically require 2 years of ITR (BIR Form 1701) to verify this income, not just bank statements.

OFW

Foreign-sourced income is exempt from Philippine income tax entirely (NIRC Sec. 23(C)) — but that exemption is a Philippine tax rule, not a global one. An OFW working in Japan, Germany, or elsewhere still pays that country's own income tax before the money ever gets remitted. The number that matters for a real DSR calculation is take-home pay after the host country's tax, not the pre-tax foreign payslip figure.

The thresholds that actually gate approval

As a general guideline across Philippine lenders:

  • Net DSR up to ~35% — comfortably within guidelines for most lenders.
  • Net DSR 35–40% — borderline. Some lenders accept this with a strong credit history; others won't.
  • Net DSR above ~40–45% — most lenders decline at this point.

Two more things worth knowing: first, existing loans count — car loans, personal loans, credit card minimums, even SSS/Pag-IBIG salary loans all get added to the numerator, since lenders pull your full credit history, not just the new mortgage payment. Second, if your rate reprices after a fixed period (common for both bank and Pag-IBIG loans), your DSR at the repriced rate matters too — a loan that looks fine in year 1 can tip into marginal territory the moment the fixed period ends.
