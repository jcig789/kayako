Is Buying Pre-selling Property Worth the Risk?
Buying pre-selling is buying a promise — the building doesn't exist yet, and you're paying today for what a developer says it'll be in one to two years. That can be a genuinely good deal, or it can go badly. The difference is due diligence.
Home & Condo September 19, 2026

What "pre-selling" actually means

A developer offers units before construction is finished — sometimes before it's even started. You reserve and start paying now; the unit typically turns over one to two years later. The appeal is real (more on that below), but so is the fact that you're paying for something that doesn't exist yet.

The real risks

Lack of visibility

The riskiest projects are the ones where all you can see is empty land or a rendering. No visible construction progress means no proof the project is actually moving.

Contract disclaimers

Watch for clauses like "subject to change without prior notice" or "price is not final." These can mean the floor plan, materials, or even the final price shift after you've already started paying.

Delays

The biggest practical downside: turnover slips, often because of funding problems or contractor disputes. A delay doesn't just mean waiting longer — it can mean paying rent and mortgage-equivalent installments simultaneously for months or years longer than planned.

Your due diligence checklist

  • Check the developer's track record. Have they actually completed and turned over projects before, on schedule? A first-time developer with no completed project carries meaningfully more risk than one with a decade of delivered buildings.
  • Verify the License to Sell (LTS). Nothing is legal without it. Under PD 957, any pre-selling offer — including reservations — legally requires a valid LTS. You can verify a project's LTS status through DHSUD (formerly HLURB).
  • Check SEC registration. Confirm the company is registered as a legitimate corporation, not just a marketing entity reselling someone else's project.
  • Inspect the title. Ask for a copy of the land title. It should already be subdivided into blocks and lots (or the condominium's mother title) and registered under the developer's or landowner's name. No title, no project — that's a major red flag regardless of how good the marketing looks.
If a developer takes your money without a valid LTS, that sale is illegal under PD 957 — but a refund isn't automatic just because the LTS is missing. You'd typically need to file a complaint with DHSUD (or pursue it legally) to actually recover what you paid. Verify the LTS before paying a reservation fee — it's far simpler to check first than to fight for a refund after.

Why buy pre-selling anyway?

Lower prices. Pre-selling units are commonly 30–50% cheaper than a comparable move-in-ready unit in the same development, since you're absorbing construction-timeline risk in exchange for the discount.

First pick of units. Early buyers get first choice of floor, view, and position within the development — the best units are usually gone well before turnover.

How this connects to your financing

Pre-selling purchases are usually structured as a Contract to Sell, with your reservation fee and down payment ("equity") paid to the developer in installments — sometimes over 24–60 months — before a bank or Pag-IBIG loan is even applied for. The bank/Pag-IBIG loan typically only gets released at or near turnover, once there's a completed unit to actually mortgage.

Practically: if you're comparing a pre-selling unit against a ready-for-occupancy one, run both prices through the simulator. The pre-selling price will likely show a smaller loan and better DSR today — just remember the calculator assumes a completed property ready to finance now, not a multi-year construction timeline with its own delay risk on top.

If you're not comfortable with the risk, the safer path is either waiting until a project is further along, or sticking to developers with a long, verifiable track record of completed, on-time turnovers. Until turnover happens, it's still a promise, not a property.
