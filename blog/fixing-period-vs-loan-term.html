Loan Term vs. Fixing Period: Why Your Rate Isn't Locked for 30 Years
Two of the most confused terms in Philippine home financing, and the choice that quietly decides how predictable your monthly payment stays over the life of the loan.
Home & Condo September 19, 2026

Two separate decisions

Loan term is the total time you have to pay off the loan — 15, 20, 25, or 30 years.

Fixing period (also called the repricing period) is a completely separate choice: how long your interest rate specifically stays locked before the bank or Pag-IBIG resets it. You can have a 30-year loan term with a rate that's only locked for the first 1, 3, or 5 years of it.

This is the single most common source of "wait, why did my payment go up?" surprises — the loan term didn't change, but the rate did, because the fixing period quietly ran out.

What actually happens when it reprices

When your fixing period ends, the bank or Pag-IBIG sends a repricing notice (banks typically 30–60 days ahead) with a new rate based on market conditions at that point. That new rate then generally holds until the next scheduled repricing — it's a periodic reset, not a rate that drifts continuously day to day the way people sometimes assume "variable rate" means.

Some banks separately offer a true floating/variable-rate product as an alternative to a fixed-then-repriced structure — worth asking your bank which one you're actually being offered, since the two behave differently.

Why shorter fixing periods look cheaper upfront

Banks and Pag-IBIG generally price shorter fixing periods lower than longer ones — a 1-year or 3-year fix will usually show a more attractive headline rate than a 10-year or 15-year fix. That's not a discount, it's a trade: you're taking on the risk that the rate might be higher next time it resets, in exchange for a lower rate today.

Play that forward and the shape is intuitive: a short fixing period can mean several repricing events over the life of a long loan term, each one a fresh roll of the dice on where rates are at that point. A long fixing period trades a higher starting rate for not having to take that roll for years at a stretch.

Refinancing is the other lever

You're not required to just accept whatever rate a repricing notice offers. If a better rate or promo becomes available elsewhere, you can refinance — pay off the existing loan with a new one, effectively resetting the whole structure (new fixing period, sometimes new term). Worth comparing against just accepting the repriced rate, especially if the gap is large.

Choosing based on risk tolerance

  • Shorter fixing period — lower starting rate, but your monthly payment can rise (sometimes more than once) over the loan's life. Better suited if you have room in your budget to absorb an increase, or if you plan to sell/refinance before the first reprice anyway.
  • Longer fixing period — higher starting rate, but a predictable payment for years. Better suited if you're budgeting tightly and a payment increase would genuinely strain you.
