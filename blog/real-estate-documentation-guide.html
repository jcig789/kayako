Real Estate Documentation Guide in the Philippines
Buying property here means a lot of paperwork, and each document means something different — some are just expressions of interest, others are the moment you're legally committed. Here's the actual order you'll encounter them in.
Home & Condo September 19, 2026

1. Preliminary stage

Letter of Intent (LOI)

A simple, informal document expressing your interest — your offer price and proposed payment terms. Generally not legally binding on either side (except sometimes an exclusivity clause, if included); either party can still walk away.

Offer to Purchase

More formal than an LOI, and this is where things start to bind: once the seller accepts it, both sides are generally committed to those terms. Read it as carefully as you'd read the Contract to Sell — by the time you're signing this, you should already mean it.

Buyer's Information Sheet (BIS)

Mainly used when buying directly from a developer. You disclose personal and financial details so the developer can assess your capacity to pay — similar in spirit to what a bank asks for a loan application.

2. Reservation and agreement

Reservation Agreement / Earnest Money Agreement

You pay a fee to hold the unit under your name, commonly for around 30 days (this varies by developer/seller, not a fixed legal period). The two are worth distinguishing:

  • Reservation fee — typically non-refundable.
  • Earnest money — can be refundable if the sale falls through, but only if the agreement actually spells out the right contingencies. Read the fine print before assuming either way.

Contract to Sell (CTS)

Signed after the initial down payment, usually around 30 days in. This is the core agreement — the seller promises to sell, you promise to buy, but the title stays with the seller until you've paid in full. Review every clause on taxes, penalties, and what happens if you default on a payment, before it gets notarized. (See our terms guide for how this differs from a Deed of Absolute Sale.)

3. Financing and finalization

Letter of Guarantee (LOG)

If you're financing through a bank, this is the bank's written confirmation to the developer that your loan is approved and the bank will cover the remaining balance. (Commonly called a "loan guarantee" informally, but "Letter of Guarantee" is the actual industry term — every detail in it needs to match your Contract to Sell exactly, including both parties' names.)

Deed of Absolute Sale (DOAS)

Executed once the property is fully paid. This is the document that legally transfers ownership and is the basis for issuing a new title in your name.

Tax deadlines start counting from the notarization date on the DOAS, and there are two separate ones, not one:
  • Capital Gains Tax (CGT) — due within 30 days of notarization, conventionally the seller's obligation (BIR Form 1706).
  • Documentary Stamp Tax (DST) — a separate deadline: on or before the 5th day of the month following the month the deed was notarized. Depending on when in the month you sign, this can land sooner than the 30-day CGT deadline — don't assume "30 days" covers both.
DST (plus transfer tax and registration fees) is conventionally the buyer's cost — this is exactly what shows up in the "Est. Closing Costs" breakdown on the KayaKo simulator's results page.

Certificate of Title

The actual proof of ownership, issued after the DOAS is processed at the Registry of Deeds: a Transfer Certificate of Title (TCT) for a house-and-lot, or a Condominium Certificate of Title (CCT) for a condo unit.

Tax Declaration

Requested from the local Assessor's Office once you have the new title — you'll submit a certified copy of the title plus photos of the property. This sets the assessed value used to compute your annual real property tax going forward.

Every one of these documents has a cost or a deadline attached — the KayaKo simulator already factors DST, transfer tax, registration, and notarial fees into your closing-cost estimate, so you know roughly what to set aside before you're standing in front of a DOAS.
